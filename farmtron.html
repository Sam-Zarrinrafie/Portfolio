Engineering Case Study · 2025–2026

Farmtron

An automated CoreXY farming platform developed through three prototype generations.

Role
Robotics and Electronics Lead Engineer
Organization
STEM MINDS CORP
Scope
Electronics, controls, software, integration

System Overview

A complete robotics platform for automated plant care and engineering education.

Farmtron combined a CoreXY gantry with a vertical tool axis to position interchangeable tools over a growing area. The system supported operations such as targeted watering and soil measurement while providing a platform for students to study motion control, electronics, software, sensing, and AI through one working machine.

My work joined the physical and digital parts of the platform: custom electronics, microcontroller firmware, a touchscreen Linux environment, an optical docking system, remote access, and an LLM-based interface that translated natural-language requests into constrained machine functions.

Key Engineering System

Optical docking that corrects mechanical positioning error.

The docking system was designed to achieve repeatable tool alignment without requiring the gantry and printed mechanical parts to provide the same level of precision. Two inexpensive light sensors measured the tracking LED from opposite sides of the tool interface. The controller used their difference to determine which direction to correct, then approached the optical center under closed-loop control.

Each measurement cycle sampled the sensors with the LED off and again with it on. Subtracting the two readings isolated the light introduced by the machine, allowing the system to reject changing room light even though it used ordinary visible-light components.

A visible LED was selected instead of infrared deliberately. It gave students and operators an immediate visual indication that optical tracking was active, making the sensing process easier to understand while retaining a low-cost and effective alignment method.

  • Accuracy: closed-loop optical centering reduced dependence on mechanical tolerance.
  • Ambient-light rejection: paired LED-off and LED-on samples isolated the machine’s own signal.
  • Cost and clarity: common light sensors and a visible LED made the system economical and observable.

Development Process

Three prototypes, each resolving a different class of problem.

Prototype 01

Mechanical proof of concept

The first prototype established the CoreXY motion layout, vertical tool axis, belt routing, and overall working envelope. Its MDF enclosure and printed components provided a fast, low-cost platform for verifying movement and tool access before investing in a more rigid structure.

Prototype 02

Rigid frame and subsystem development

The second prototype moved to a modular aluminum extrusion frame and became the main integration testbed. This generation supported rapid work on optical docking, electronics, tool handling, fluid delivery, and coordinated motion while keeping components accessible for measurement and revision.

Prototype 03

Integrated product prototype

The third prototype consolidated the system into a cleaner product architecture. It brought together the growing bed, integrated enclosure, touchscreen computer, purpose-built control electronics, automated tooling, and plant-care functions. The result was a platform suitable for continued development, demonstrations, and education.

Technical Architecture

High-level computing with deterministic embedded control.

01

Motion and safety firmware

Microcontroller firmware handled CoreXY kinematics, homing, endstops, coordinated motion, emergency stopping, sensor acquisition, and tool operations. Time-critical motion remained local to the controller.

02

Custom control electronics

A purpose-built PCB organized motor drivers, pumps, sensors, switches, and auxiliary connections around the machine’s actual I/O requirements. The board reduced wiring complexity and made the system easier to assemble and service.

03

Linux operating environment

A custom Debian-based environment on the Arduino UNO Q hosted the touchscreen interface, device services, and higher-level application logic while communicating with the real-time microcontroller.

04

AI-assisted operation

Language-model and speech interfaces converted operator requests into a defined set of machine tools. The AI layer worked through controlled commands instead of directly generating low-level motor movement.

05

Remote operation

Authenticated device pairing, commands, status, and software delivery connected the machine to a hosted control service. Signed manifests, integrity checks, immutable artifacts, and rollback supported safer updates.

06

Designed for observation

The interface and hardware exposed machine state clearly so learners could connect software decisions with physical behavior. The visible optical-tracking LED is one example of this design approach.
